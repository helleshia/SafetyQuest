Module 12: Basic First Aid  1. INTRODUCTION Brief Introduction to the Topic Accidents	and	emergencies	can	happen	almost	anywhere,	at	home,	in	school,	during	sports	activities,	while	travelling,	or	in	the	community.	A	person	may	suddenly	experience	a	cut,	burn,	 fall,	 choking	 incident,	 fainting	 episode,	 or	 other	 injury.	In	these	 situations,	 knowing	what	to	do	in	the	first	few	minutes	can	make	a	big	difference.		First	aid	refers	to	the	immediate	and	temporary	care	given	to	a	person	who	is	injured	or	suddenly	becomes	ill	while	waiting	for	professional	medical	assistance.	First	aid	does	not	replace	treatment	from	doctors,	nurses,	emergency	medical	responders,	or	other	trained	professionals.	Instead,	it	aims	to	keep	the	situation	from	becoming	worse,	provide	appropriate	immediate	care,	and	help	the	person	until	professional	help	becomes	available.		The	first-aid	provider	must	also	remember	an	important	principle:	your	own	safety	comes	first.	A	person	cannot	effectively	help	an	injured	individual	if	the	situation	also	puts	the	rescuer	in	danger.	The	basic	emergency	sequence	is	therefore	to	Check,	Call,	and	Care:	check	the	scene	and	person,	call	for	appropriate	emergency	assistance,	and	provide	care	according	to	the	condition	and	the	rescuer's	level	of	training.		Why	Basic	First	Aid	Is	Important		Learning	basic	first	aid	is	important	because	emergencies	do	not	always	happen	when	medical	professionals	are	immediately	present.	A	person	who	knows	basic	first	aid	may	be	able	to	recognize	danger,	seek	help	quickly,	provide	appropriate	immediate	care,	and	prevent	additional	harm.		First	aid	also	teaches	learners	to	remain	calm	and	make	responsible	decisions	during	stressful	situations.	Instead	of	panicking,	learners	can	follow	simple	steps:	make	the	area	safe,	assess	the	situation,	ask	for	help,	and	provide	appropriate	care.		The	goal	is	not	to	make	learners	act	like	doctors	or	nurses.	The	goal	is	to	help	them	understand	what	they	can	safely	do,	what	they	should	not	do,	and	when	professional	help	is	necessary.		Age-Appropriate	Explanation	for	Learners		Think	of	first	aid	as	helping	someone	safely	until	the	right	help	arrives.

	For	example:		Your	classmate	falls	during	a	school	activity	and	gets	a	bleeding	cut.	What	should	you	do?		You	should	not	immediately	panic	or	run	toward	the	person	without	looking	around.	First,	check	whether	the	area	is	safe.	Then	ask	the	person	what	happened,	get	an	adult	or	teacher,	and	call	emergency	help	if	needed.	If	it	is	a	minor	wound	and	you	are	trained	and	it	is	safe	to	do	so,	appropriate	first	aid	may	be	provided.		Remember:		STOP	→	CHECK	→	CALL	→	CARE		2.	LEARNING	OBJECTIVES		At	the	end	of	this	module,	learners	should	be	able	to:		Objective	1		Define	basic	first	aid	and	explain	its	purpose	in	responding	to	injuries	and	sudden	illnesses.		Objective	2		Identify	common	emergencies	and	injuries	that	may	require	immediate	first-aid	attention,	such	as	bleeding,	burns,	choking,	fainting,	and	possible	fractures.		Objective	3		Demonstrate	or	describe	appropriate	first-aid	actions	while	considering	personal	safety,	the	condition	of	the	injured	person,	and	the	learner's	level	of	training. Objective	5		Apply	the	principles	of	Check,	Call,	and	Care	when	responding	to	a	basic	emergency	situation.

	Objective	6		Identify	unsafe	first-aid	practices	and	explain	why	certain	actions	should	be	avoided.		3.	INTERACTIVE	LESSON	A.	What	Is	First	Aid?		First	aid	is	the	immediate	assistance	provided	to	a	person	who	has	been	injured	or	suddenly	becomes	ill	before	professional	medical	care	is	available.		The	main	purposes	of	first	aid	are	to:		Protect	the	person	from	further	harm.	Recognize	serious	or	life-threatening	conditions.	Provide	appropriate	immediate	care.	Obtain	professional	medical	assistance	when	needed.	Monitor	the	person's	condition	while	waiting	for	help.		First	aid	should	always	be	appropriate	to	the	situation	and	to	the	helper's	training.		Remember:		First	aid	is	not	about	doing	everything.		It	is	about	doing	the	right	thing	safely.		B.	The	Three	Basic	Emergency	Actions:	CHECK,	CALL,	CARE	STEP	1:	CHECK		Before	helping,	check	the	scene	for	safety.		Ask	yourself:	Is	there	traffic?	Is	there	fire	or	smoke?	Is	there	electricity?	Are	there	dangerous	chemicals?	Could	the	person	or	rescuer	be	exposed	to	another	hazard?	Is	the	area	safe	enough	to	approach?		

--- PAGE 4 ---
Do	not	rush	into	a	dangerous	situation.		After	checking	the	scene,	determine	whether	the	person	is	responsive	and	whether	there	are	obvious	life-threatening	conditions.	For	an	apparently	unresponsive	person,	the	Red	Cross	recommends	checking	responsiveness,	breathing,	and	life-threatening	bleeding	or	other	obvious	life	threats.	The	responsiveness	check	should	take	no	more	than	10	seconds.		Simple	learner	reminder:		LOOK	→	THINK	→	THEN	HELP		STEP	2:	CALL		If	the	situation	is	serious,	get	emergency	assistance	immediately.		A	learner	should:		Call	the	local	emergency	number	when	appropriate.	Alert	a	teacher,	parent,	school	nurse,	security	personnel,	or	other	responsible	adult.	Ask	another	person	to	call	while	you	remain	with	the	injured	person,	when	possible.	Clearly	explain	what	happened	and	where	the	emergency	occurred.	Follow	the	instructions	provided	by	the	emergency	dispatcher.		In	the	Philippines,	learners	should	know	the	emergency	contact	procedures	established	by	their	school,	community,	or	locality.		Remember:		Do	not	be	afraid	to	ask	for	immediate	help.		Calling	for	help	is	part	of	first	aid.		STEP	3:	CARE		After	checking	the	situation	and	calling	for	help	when	needed,	provide	care	that	is	appropriate	to	the	person's	condition	and	consistent	with	your	training.	Continue	observing	the	person	while	waiting	for	professional	assistance.		The	 American	 Red	 Cross	 emphasizes	 that	 care	 should	 be	 provided	 according	 to	 the	condition	found	and	the	rescuer's	level	of	training.

	C.	FIRST	AID	FOR	BLEEDING		A	wound	is	an	injury	in	which	the	skin	or	other	body	tissues	are	damaged.	Open	wounds	may	include	abrasions,	lacerations,	avulsions,	and	puncture	wounds.	Some	bleeding	can	be	minor,	while	severe	bleeding	can	become	life-threatening.		Common	Examples	Scrape	from	falling	Cut	from	a	sharp	object	Puncture	wound	Deep	wound	Severe	bleeding	following	an	accident	Basic	Response	to	Minor	Bleeding		If	the	situation	is	safe	and	the	injury	is	minor:		Protect	yourself	from	contact	with	blood	when	possible.	Use	appropriate	protective	barriers	such	as	gloves.	Apply	direct	pressure	to	the	wound	with	clean	gauze	or	a	clean	dressing.	Maintain	pressure	until	bleeding	is	controlled.	Seek	medical	attention	when	the	wound	is	serious	or	requires	further	assessment.		For	life-threatening	bleeding,	emergency	medical	assistance	should	be	obtained	immediately.	Direct	pressure	is	an	important	first-aid	measure	for	controlling	severe	external	bleeding.		Do	Not:	Ignore	heavy	or	continuous	bleeding.	Remove	an	embedded	object	from	a	wound.	Touch	blood	unnecessarily.	Delay	emergency	assistance	when	bleeding	appears	life-threatening.	D.	FIRST	AID	FOR	BURNS		A	burn	can	occur	because	of	heat,	chemicals,	electricity,	radiation,	or	other	sources.	Burns	vary	in	severity,	and	some	require	immediate	medical	treatment.		Common	Causes	Hot	water	Steam

Fire	Hot	cooking	equipment	Chemicals	Electricity	Basic	First	Aid	for	a	Minor	Thermal	Burn	Move	away	from	the	source	of	heat	if	it	is	safe.	Remove	clothing	or	jewelry	near	the	burned	area	if	it	is	not	stuck	to	the	skin.	Cool	the	burn	using	clean,	cool	running	water.	Continue	cooling	for	an	appropriate	period.	Seek	medical	evaluation	when	the	burn	is	serious	or	concerning.		The	Red	Cross	recommends	clean,	cool	running	water	for	cooling	burns	and	advises	against	applying	butter	or	cream	as	a	first-aid	treatment.		Serious	Burns		Immediate	medical	attention	may	be	necessary	for	burns	that	are	deep,	extensive,	involve	sensitive	areas	such	as	the	face	or	hands,	or	result	from	electrical	or	chemical	exposure.		Important:		Do	not	put	butter,	toothpaste,	oil,	or	other	household	substances	on	a	serious	burn.		E.	FIRST	AID	FOR	CHOKING		Choking	occurs	when	an	object	blocks	a	person's	airway.		Possible	Signs	of	Serious	Choking		A	person	may:		§ Be	unable	to	speak.	§ Be	unable	to	cough	effectively.	§ Have	dif_iculty	breathing.	§ Make	unusual	or	high-pitched	sounds.	§ Hold	the	throat.	§ Become	pale	or	bluish.	§ Become	unresponsive.

If	a	person	can	speak	or	cough	forcefully,	encourage	them	to	continue	coughing	while	remaining	ready	to	respond	if	their	condition	worsens.	A	person	who	cannot	speak,	cry,	or	cough	effectively	requires	immediate	action	and	emergency	assistance.		Adult	or	Child	Choking		For	trained	responders,	current	first-aid	guidance	includes	alternating	five	back	blows	and	five	abdominal	thrusts	for	a	conscious	adult	or	child	with	severe	choking.	Specific	procedures	differ	for	infants	and	certain	special	situations,	so	learners	should	receive	hands-on	instruction	from	a	qualified	first-aid	instructor	rather	than	relying	only	on	written	material.		Learner	Safety	Rule		Never	practice	choking	maneuvers	on	a	classmate	for	fun.		These	techniques	should	be	demonstrated	and	practiced	only	in	an	appropriately	supervised	first-aid	training	environment.		F.	FAINTING	AND	UNRESPONSIVENESS		A	person	may	faint	because	of	several	possible	causes,	including	heat,	emotional	stress,	dehydration,	illness,	or	other	medical	conditions.		If	Someone	Faints	Check	that	the	area	is	safe.	Check	whether	the	person	responds.	Get	an	adult	or	emergency	assistance.	Check	breathing.	Do	not	allow	the	person	to	immediately	stand	up	if	they	are	still	weak	or	unwell.	Continue	monitoring	the	person.		If	a	person	is	unresponsive	and	not	breathing	normally	or	is	only	gasping,	this	is	a	medical	emergency.	Emergency	services	should	be	activated	and	CPR/AED	procedures	should	be	started	by	someone	trained	to	do	so.		Important:		Do	not	give	food	or	drink	to	an	unconscious	person.

G.	SUSPECTED	FRACTURES	AND	SERIOUS	INJURIES		A	fracture	is	a	break	or	crack	in	a	bone.		Possible	Signs	Severe	pain	Swelling	Bruising	Difficulty	moving	the	affected	body	part	Abnormal	shape	or	position	Inability	to	use	the	body	part	normally	What	to	Do	Keep	the	person	still.	Do	not	attempt	to	straighten	the	injured	body	part.	Get	professional	medical	assistance.	Avoid	unnecessary	movement,	particularly	when	a	head,	neck,	or	spinal	injury	is	suspected.		The	Red	Cross	advises	not	asking	a	person	to	move	when	a	head,	neck,	or	spinal	injury	is	suspected	unless	movement	is	necessary	for	safety,	CPR,	or	bleeding	control.		H.	SHOCK		Shock	is	a	serious	condition	that	can	occur	when	the	body	is	not	receiving	adequate	circulation	to	its	vital	organs.		Possible	signs	can	include:		Pale	or	cool	skin	Weakness	Rapid	breathing	Confusion	Restlessness	Dizziness	Fainting	Basic	Response	Call	for	emergency	medical	assistance.	Keep	the	person	appropriately	positioned.	Keep	them	from	becoming	too	cold	or	overheated.	Monitor	their	condition.	Do	not	give	food	or	drink	unless	directed	by	medical	professionals.

	The	Red	Cross	recommends	monitoring	and	continuing	care	while	waiting	for	emergency	medical	services.		I.	CPR	AND	AED:	KNOW	WHEN	TO	GET	HELP		CPR	stands	for	cardiopulmonary	resuscitation.		It	is	used	when	a	person	is	experiencing	cardiac	arrest.		Learners	should	understand	the	important	warning	sign:		An	unresponsive	person	who	is	not	breathing	normally	or	is	only	gasping	needs	immediate	emergency	assistance.		For	trained	individuals,	CPR	should	be	started	and	an	AED	used	as	soon	as	possible	when	appropriate.		Because	CPR	and	AED	use	are	hands-on	skills,	learners	should	receive	practical	training	from	qualified	instructors.	Reading	about	CPR	alone	does	not	provide	sufficient	skill	for	certification.		J.	WHEN	SHOULD	YOU	GET	PROFESSIONAL	HELP?		Get	emergency	assistance	immediately	when	a	person:		Is	unresponsive.	Is	not	breathing	normally	or	is	only	gasping.	Has	life-threatening	bleeding.	Has	severe	difficulty	breathing.	Has	a	serious	burn.	Has	a	suspected	serious	head,	neck,	or	spinal	injury.	Has	severe	choking.	Has	a	serious	injury	after	a	major	accident.	Has	another	obvious	life-threatening	condition.		When	in	doubt,	get	help	rather	than	assuming	that	the	condition	is	minor.		INTERACTIVE	SCENARIO	“What	Would	You	Do?”

Module 13: Bullying Awareness  1. INTRODUCTION Brief Introduction to the Topic Bullying	is	a	serious	concern	that	can	affect	learners	in	school,	at	home,	online,	and	in	other	places	where	young	people	interact.	It	occurs	when	a	person	or	group	repeatedly	uses	words,	actions,	social	behavior,	or	digital	communication	to	hurt,	intimidate,	embarrass,	exclude,	or	control	another	person.	Bullying	can	affect	a	learner's	feelings,	relationships,	participation	in	school,	sense	of	safety,	and	overall	well-being.		Bullying	is	not	simply	a	disagreement	between	friends	or	an	occasional	misunderstanding.	It	becomes	particularly	concerning	when	harmful	behavior	is	repeated	or	is	part	of	a	pattern	of	behavior	in	which	one	person	or	group	uses	social,	physical,	verbal,	or	digital	power	against	another	person.	Even	when	the	person	causing	harm	says	that	they	were	"only	joking,"	the	effect	on	the	other	learner	still	matters.		Bullying	can	take	different	forms.	Physical	bullying	may	include	hitting,	kicking,	pushing,	tripping,	damaging	belongings,	or	other	unwanted	physical	actions.	Verbal	bullying	may	include	repeated	name-calling,	insults,	threats,	mocking,	or	hurtful	comments.	Social	or	relational	bullying	may	involve	deliberately	excluding	someone,	spreading	rumors,	embarrassing	someone,	manipulating	friendships,	or	encouraging	others	not	to	associate	with	a	particular	learner.	Cyberbullying	involves	harmful	behavior	carried	out	through	digital	platforms	such	as	social	media,	messaging	applications,	online	games,	group	chats,	or	other	electronic	communication.		Why	Bullying	Awareness	Is	Important		Bullying	awareness	helps	learners	recognize	unsafe	or	harmful	behavior	before	it	becomes	more	serious.	When	learners	understand	what	bullying	looks	like,	they	are	better	prepared	to	identify	situations	that	require	help,	respond	safely,	support	classmates,	and	report	concerns	to	trusted	adults.		Awareness	also	helps	create	a	school	environment	where	learners	understand	that	being	different	is	not	a	reason	for	mistreatment.	Differences	in	appearance,	personality,	interests,	abilities,	family	background,	culture,	socioeconomic	circumstances,	academic	performance,	or	other	characteristics	should	not	be	used	as	reasons	to	humiliate	or	exclude	someone.		Learning	about	bullying	also	teaches	an	important	principle:	everyone	has	a	responsibility	to	contribute	to	a	safe	and	respectful	learning	environment.	Learners	who	experience	

--- PAGE 2 ---
bullying	should	know	that	asking	for	help	is	not	a	sign	of	weakness.	Learners	who	witness	bullying	can	also	play	an	important	role	by	avoiding	participation,	supporting	the	affected	person,	and	seeking	help	from	a	trusted	adult.		Age-Appropriate	Explanation	for	Learners		For	younger	learners,	bullying	can	be	explained	simply:		"Bullying	is	when	someone	repeatedly	hurts,	scares,	embarrasses,	or	leaves	another	person	out	on	purpose.	It	can	happen	with	words,	actions,	relationships,	or	through	the	internet.	If	something	makes	you	feel	unsafe	or	repeatedly	hurts	you	or	someone	else,	tell	a	trusted	adult."		Older	learners	can	be	introduced	to	more	complex	situations,	including	cyberbullying,	group	pressure,	social	exclusion,	rumors,	anonymous	online	accounts,	harmful	posts,	and	the	responsibility	of	bystanders.		The	most	important	lesson	is	that	no	learner	deserves	to	be	bullied,	and	learners	should	not	be	expected	to	handle	serious	bullying	situations	alone.		2. 3.	INTERACTIVE	LESSON	Lesson	1:	What	Is	Bullying?	Bullying	 is	 harmful	 behavior	 that	 can	 occur	 between	 learners	 or	 within	 groups.	 It	 may	happen	 in	 classrooms,	 hallways,	 playgrounds,	 transportation	 areas,	 communities,	 social	media	platforms,	online	games,	messaging	applications,	or	other	environments.		A	useful	way	to	understand	bullying	is	to	examine	three	important	elements:		1.	Harmful	Behavior		The	behavior	causes	or	is	intended	to	cause	physical,	emotional,	social,	or	psychological	harm.		2.	Repetition	or	Pattern	Bullying	commonly	involves	repeated	behavior	or	a	continuing	pattern.	However,	a	single	serious	incident	should	never	simply	be	ignored	because	it	happened	only	once.	Threats,	violence,	sexual	harassment,	serious	humiliation,	or	other	dangerous	behavior	should	be	reported	and	addressed	even	when	there	is	no	established	pattern.		3.	Power	or	Difficulty	Defending	Oneself		Bullying	may	involve	an	imbalance	of	power.	The	imbalance	may	be	physical,	social,	emotional,	digital,	or	related	to	popularity,	group	membership,	influence,	or	access	to	information.		Remember:		Not	every	conflict	is	bullying.	Two	learners	may	have	an	argument	because	they	disagree	about	 a	 game,	 group	 activity,	 friendship	 issue,	 or	 classroom	 task.	 Such	 a	 conflict	 may	 still	require	 guidance	 and	 resolution,	 but	 it	 does	 not	 automatically	 mean	 that	 bullying	 has	occurred.	The	 appropriate	 response	 should	 focus	 on	 what	 happened,	 how	 serious	 it	 was,	whether	there	is	a	pattern,	whether	there	is	a	power	imbalance,	and	whether	someone	is	being	placed	at	risk. Module 14: Water Safety  1. INTRODUCTION Brief Introduction to the Topic (Understanding Water Safety) Water is an important part of everyday life. People use water for drinking, bathing, recreation, swimming, transportation, fishing, sports, and many other activities. Swimming and other water activities can be enjoyable and beneficial, but water can also become dangerous when safety rules are ignored or when a person enters an environment beyond their swimming ability. Water safety refers to the knowledge, skills, behaviors, and precautions used to prevent drowning, injuries, and other water-related emergencies. Water safety applies not only to swimming pools but also to beaches, rivers, lakes, ponds, waterfalls, canals, boats, and other bodies of water.  A person does not need to be a weak swimmer to be at risk. Strong swimmers can also encounter unexpected hazards such as deep water, strong currents, waves, slippery surfaces, sudden changes in water depth, poor visibility, exhaustion, or equipment failure.  Why Water Safety Is Important  Drowning can happen quickly and quietly. A person in distress may not always be able to shout for help or make obvious movements. For this reason, prevention, close supervision, and recognizing danger are extremely important. Children and young learners are particularly vulnerable around water because they may not fully understand water hazards or may overestimate their swimming ability. Young learners may also be attracted to water and may approach pools, rivers, beaches, or other bodies of water without recognizing the potential danger.  Water safety education teaches learners to:  o Recognize dangerous water environments. o Follow rules established by adults and lifeguards. o Understand their own swimming abilities and limitations. o Use appropriate safety equipment. o Avoid entering unsafe water. o Respond appropriately when someone is in trouble. o Ask responsible adults for help.   

--- PAGE 2 ---
Age-Appropriate Explanation for Learners  For younger learners, water safety can be explained simply:  "Water can be fun, but water can also be dangerous. Older learners can understand additional concepts such as currents, water depth, weather conditions, swimming ability, life jackets, lifeguards, emergency response, and the dangers of attempting an unsafe rescue. Module 15: Poison, Household Hazards & Electricity Safety  1. INTRODUCTION Brief Introduction to the Topic  Our homes are places where we live, learn, play, eat, and rest. Although the home is usually a safe place, there are many objects and substances inside it that can become dangerous when they are used incorrectly. Cleaning products, medicines, pesticides, cosmetics, batteries, sharp objects, hot appliances, electrical outlets, cords, and other household materials can cause injuries or poisoning when they are mishandled. Poison, Household Hazards & Electricity Safety teaches learners how to recognize potentially dangerous substances, objects, and situations at home. The module focuses on three important areas: poison safety, general household hazard awareness, and electricity safety. Learners will practice identifying risks and understanding what actions are safe and unsafe.  The most important lesson is simple: If something may be poisonous, dangerous, or electrical, do not experiment with it. Stop, move away, and tell a trusted adult.  Why This Topic Is Important  Children and young learners are naturally curious. They may want to open containers, taste unfamiliar substances, touch electrical equipment, or investigate objects that adults consider dangerous. Curiosity is normal, but unsafe exploration can result in poisoning, burns, electric shock, fires, cuts, falls, or other serious injuries.  Learning safety rules can help prevent accidents before they happen. Learners should understand that many dangerous substances do not always look dangerous. A liquid may look like water but may actually be a cleaning chemical. A colorful tablet may look like candy but may be medicine. An ordinary-looking electrical cord may have damaged insulation and create a shock or fire hazard. Safety education therefore helps learners develop the habit of recognizing danger, avoiding unnecessary contact, and asking a responsible adult for help.  Age-Appropriate Explanation for Learners  Think of your home as a place with safe zones and danger zones. Toys, books, and ordinary school supplies may be safe when used properly. However, medicines, cleaning products, pesticides, electrical outlets, damaged cords, sharp tools, hot surfaces, and other hazardous materials require special care. 

--- PAGE 2 ---
 You do not have to be an expert to stay safe. You only need to remember:  STOP – MOVE AWAY – TELL A TRUSTED ADULT.  If you see something that you do not recognize, do not touch, taste, smell closely, open, mix, or plug it in. Ask an adult first.  2. A poison is a substance that can cause harm if it enters the body, such as through swallowing, breathing, or contact with the eyes or skin. Some substances may be poisonous in certain amounts even if they are useful when used correctly.  Examples of potentially harmful substances include: o Medicines o Cleaning products o Bleach and disinfectants o Laundry products o Pesticides and insecticides o Some automotive products o Certain chemicals and solvents 

--- PAGE 3 ---
o Some cosmetics and personal-care products o Button or coin batteries o Unknown substances in unlabelled containers  The danger depends on the substance, amount, route of exposure, and other circumstances.  Medicines Are Not Candy  Medicine can help people when it is prescribed or used correctly. However, medicine can also be harmful when taken by the wrong person or in the wrong amount.  Learners should remember:  Medicine should only be taken when a responsible adult or healthcare professional has instructed them to take it.  Never:  Take someone else's medicine. Take extra medicine because you think it will work faster. Share prescription medicine with another person. Play with medicine bottles. Pretend medicine is candy. Take medicine without adult supervision when supervision is required. Household Chemicals  Cleaning products are designed to clean surfaces, remove stains, kill germs, or perform other specific tasks. They are not food or drinks.  Examples include:  § Toilet cleaners § Drain cleaners § Bleach § Disinfectants § Detergents § Glass cleaners § Oven cleaners § Insecticides 

--- PAGE 4 ---
 Never mix household cleaning products.  Some combinations of chemicals can produce dangerous gases or other harmful reactions. Learners should never experiment with cleaning chemicals.  Safe Storage of Poisons  Hazardous substances should remain in their original, properly labelled containers whenever possible.  Important rules include:  § Keep chemicals away from food and drinks. § Do not transfer chemicals into drinking bottles or food containers. § Keep medicines and chemicals out of children's reach. § Follow product labels and adult instructions. § Keep hazardous products properly closed when not being used. § Do not remove warning labels.  What If You Find an Unknown Substance?  If you find a bottle, container, tablet, powder, liquid, or other substance that you do not recognize:  STOP.  Do not touch or taste it.  MOVE AWAY.  Keep yourself and other children away from it.  TELL A TRUSTED ADULT.  Tell a parent, guardian, teacher, or another responsible adult.  B. WHAT TO DO IF SOMEONE MAY HAVE BEEN POISONED A suspected poisoning can be an emergency.  

--- PAGE 5 ---
If someone has swallowed, inhaled, or been exposed to a potentially poisonous substance:  Tell a trusted adult immediately. Keep the product container or label available for the responsible adult if it can be done safely. Contact appropriate emergency or poison-control services according to local procedures. Follow instructions from qualified medical or poison-control professionals. Do not make the person vomit unless a qualified professional specifically instructs you to do so. Do not give food, drink, or another substance as a home remedy unless instructed by an appropriate healthcare professional. If the person is unconscious, having difficulty breathing, having seizures, or showing severe symptoms, emergency medical assistance should be obtained immediately. Important Learner Reminder  A child should not try to treat poisoning alone.  The correct response is to get an adult and professional help as quickly as possible.  C. HOUSEHOLD HAZARDS A household hazard is anything in or around the home that can potentially cause injury, illness, or damage when it is used, stored, or handled unsafely.  Common Household Hazards 1. Slips and Falls  Wet floors, loose rugs, clutter, toys on stairs, and objects left in walkways can cause people to fall. Safety behaviour: Keep walkways clear. Walk rather than run indoors. Report wet floors. Use stairs carefully. Keep toys and belongings organized. 2. Sharp Objects  Knives, scissors, razors, broken glass, needles, and other sharp objects can cause cuts or puncture injuries. 

--- PAGE 6 ---
 Learners should:  Use age-appropriate tools only with proper supervision. Carry scissors safely. Never play with knives or blades. Tell an adult if glass breaks. Never pick up broken glass with bare hands. 3. Hot Objects and Surfaces  Stoves, ovens, irons, hot liquids, cookware, heaters, and other heated objects can cause burns.  Remember:  Hot things can hurt even when they do not look dangerous.  Children should ask adults for help with hot appliances and cooking equipment.  4. Fire Hazards  Matches, lighters, candles, cooking equipment, overloaded electrical outlets, and damaged electrical equipment can contribute to fires.  Learners should never use matches or lighters as toys.  If a fire starts:  Get away, alert others, and call for emergency help.  Do not hide from a fire and do not return to a burning building.  5. Small Objects and Batteries  Small objects may present a choking hazard to young children. Button or coin batteries are particularly dangerous if swallowed and require immediate medical attention.  Never: Put batteries in your mouth. Play with batteries. 

--- PAGE 7 ---
Allow young children to handle loose button batteries. Treat batteries like toys.  If a button or coin battery may have been swallowed, tell an adult immediately and seek urgent medical advice.  D. ELECTRICITY SAFETY What Is Electricity?  Electricity provides energy for many things we use every day, including lights, televisions, computers, refrigerators, fans, chargers, and other appliances.  Electricity is useful, but it can also cause:  Electric shock Burns Fires Serious injury Death  Electricity should therefore always be treated with caution.  Electrical Outlets  Electrical outlets should never be treated as toys.  Never:  Put fingers or objects into an electrical outlet. Play with outlet covers. Insert metal objects into outlets. Pull on electrical cords. Touch outlets with wet hands.  Young children should have electrical outlets appropriately protected according to safety guidance.  Electrical Cords Check cords for visible damage such as:  

--- PAGE 8 ---
Frayed wires Cracked insulation Exposed wires Burn marks Loose plugs  If you notice a damaged cord:  Do not touch or use it. Tell an adult.  Never Pull a Plug by the Cord  When unplugging an appliance, adults should grasp the plug rather than pulling the cord.  Repeatedly pulling on a cord can damage it and increase the risk of electrical injury or fire.  E. WATER AND ELECTRICITY DO NOT MIX  Water conducts electricity, creating an increased risk of electric shock.  Never handle electrical equipment with wet hands.  Keep electrical appliances away from:  Bathtubs Sinks Showers Pools Wet floors Other sources of water  If an electrical appliance falls into water, do not reach into the water to retrieve it. Move away and tell an adult immediately. A qualified person should handle the situation safely. F. ELECTRICAL APPLIANCES  Electrical appliances should be used according to their instructions and appropriate age requirements. 

--- PAGE 9 ---
 Learners should:  Use appliances only when permitted and supervised. Keep liquids away from electrical equipment. Keep cords away from hot surfaces. Turn off equipment when instructed. Tell an adult if an appliance smells unusual, sparks, overheats, or makes an unexpected sound.  Do not attempt to repair electrical equipment yourself.  G. OVERLOADING OUTLETS  Plugging too many appliances into one outlet or power strip can create a fire or electrical hazard, depending on the equipment and electrical system.  Learners should never decide on their own how many appliances can safely be connected.  If an outlet or extension cord appears overloaded: Do not touch or rearrange it. Tell an adult. Adults should follow manufacturer instructions and appropriate electrical safety standards.  H. WHAT IF SOMEONE RECEIVES AN ELECTRIC SHOCK?  Electric shock can be serious even when the person appears to recover.  If someone is being shocked: Do not touch the person while they may still be in contact with the electrical source. Alert an adult immediately. Have the electricity disconnected safely if this can be done without exposing anyone else to danger. Call emergency medical services when appropriate. Do not put yourself at risk trying to rescue someone.  If the person is unconscious or not breathing normally after the electrical danger has been made safe, emergency medical assistance and appropriate first-aid/CPR procedures may be required.
