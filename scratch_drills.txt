Module 6: Earthquake and Fire Evacuation Drills
1. INTRODUCTION
Brief Introduction to the Topic
Emergencies such as earthquakes and fires can happen suddenly, often giving people
very little time to decide what to do. Because emergencies may cause confusion, fear,
smoke, falling objects, blocked pathways, or damaged structures, knowing the correct
safety procedures before an actual emergency occurs is extremely important.
An evacuation drill is a planned practice activity that teaches people how to respond
during an emergency. Earthquake drills and fire evacuation drills allow learners to practice
important actions such as recognizing an alarm, protecting themselves, following
designated evacuation routes, moving calmly to a safe location, and accounting for
everyone after evacuation.
A drill is more than simply practicing how to leave a building. It helps learners develop
knowledge, awareness, coordination, discipline, and appropriate decision-making under
stressful conditions.
Why the Topic Is Important
During an actual earthquake or fire, people may panic or become unsure about what to
do. A person who has practised the correct procedures is more likely to recognize the
emergency signal, follow instructions, avoid dangerous behavior, and move toward a
safer location.
Earthquake and fire drills are particularly important in schools, offices, public buildings,
and other places where many people occupy the same structure. Practicing together
helps learners understand evacuation routes, emergency exits, assembly areas, and the
responsibilities of teachers, staff, emergency personnel, and other designated safety
officers.
Regular drills can also help identify problems before an actual emergency occurs. For
example, a drill may reveal that an evacuation route is blocked, an exit is difficult to
access, learners do not know where the assembly area is located, or people need
clearer instructions.
Age-Appropriate Explanation for Learners
Think of an emergency drill as a practice for a real emergency.
You practice fire drills even when there is no fire. You practice earthquake procedures
even when the ground is not shaking. Why? Because practicing helps your body and
mind remember what to do.
During a drill, remember:
STOP → PROTECT → LISTEN → EVACUATE → ASSEMBLE → ACCOUNT FOR
EVERYONE
The goal is not to be the fastest person. The goal is to stay safe, follow instructions, and
help the group move safely.
2. Learning Objectives
At the end of the module, learners should be able to:
A. Explain the purpose of earthquake and fire evacuation drills and identify the
major hazards associated with earthquakes and fires.
B. Demonstrate appropriate safety procedures before, during, and after an
earthquake and fire evacuation drill.
C. Identify and use designated evacuation routes, emergency exits, assembly
areas, and other important safety facilities in their school or learning
environment.
D. Apply appropriate decision-making skills to emergency scenarios by selecting
safe actions, following instructions, and avoiding behaviors that may place
themselves or others at risk.
3. Interactive Lesson
A. What Is an Evacuation Drill?
An evacuation drill is a planned exercise designed to help people practice what
they should do during an emergency.
During a drill, learners practice:
• Recognizing an emergency alarm or signal
• Responding quickly but calmly
• Following emergency instructions
• Using designated evacuation routes
• Using stairs and emergency exits properly
• Moving toward an assembly area
• Staying with their assigned group
• Remaining at the assembly area
• Participating in headcounts or accountability procedures
Important Concept
A drill is practice for safety—not a race.
Learners should not push, run unnecessarily, shout, or compete with classmates.
The purpose is to practice safe and organized evacuation.
B. Earthquake Safety Drill
An earthquake happens when there is sudden movement of the Earth's crust,
producing ground shaking.
Earthquakes can cause:
Falling objects
Broken glass
Damaged walls or ceilings
Falling furniture
Electrical hazards
Fires
Blocked exits
Structural damage
Falling debris
Because earthquakes can happen without warning, knowing what to do
immediately is essential.
C. The “Drop, Cover, and Hold On” Procedure
When an earthquake begins and you are indoors, the standard protective action is:
1. DROP
Lower yourself to the ground so that the earthquake is less likely to knock you over.
2. COVER
Protect your head and neck and, when possible, take shelter under a sturdy desk or
table.
3. HOLD ON
Hold onto the desk or table and be prepared to move with it until the shaking stops.
Simple Memory Guide
DROP → COVER → HOLD ON
Why Is This Important?
During an earthquake, many injuries can result from falling objects, broken
materials, or people losing their balance. Protecting the head and neck and taking
shelter can reduce exposure to these hazards.
D. Earthquake Drill: Step-by-Step Procedure
Step 1: Recognize the Earthquake Signal
When shaking begins, or the earthquake drill signal is given:
Stay calm and immediately perform the appropriate protective action.
Do not immediately rush toward the door.
Step 2: Drop, Cover, and Hold On
If indoors:
o Drop to the ground.
o Cover your head and neck.
o Take cover under a sturdy desk or table when available.
o Hold onto the shelter.
o Stay away from windows and objects that could fall.
Example: A learner is sitting inside a classroom when the earthquake alarm begins.
Unsafe response:
The learner immediately runs toward the door.
Safer response:
The learner drops, takes cover, and holds on until the shaking stops or the designated
drill instruction changes.
E. After the Shaking Stops
Once the shaking has stopped and the teacher or designated safety personnel
instructs to evacuate:
o Remain calm.
o Listen carefully to instructions.
o Leave using the designated evacuation route.
o Avoid damaged areas.
o Do not use elevators where evacuation procedures prohibit their use.
o Use stairs carefully.
o Do not push or overtake others.
o Proceed to the designated assembly area.
Important:
Do not evacuate by running immediately during the shaking unless there is an
immediate life-threatening condition requiring another response.
The initial priority is to protect yourself from falling objects and debris.
F. Fire Safety Drill
A fire can spread quickly and may produce heat, smoke, flames, and toxic gases.
Fire emergencies may occur because of:
Electrical problems
Cooking accidents
Flammable materials
Improper use of equipment
Open flames
Chemical or laboratory accidents
Other ignition sources
A fire drill teaches learners how to respond when a fire alarm sounds or when an
evacuation order is given.
G. Recognizing a Fire Emergency
A learner may become aware of a fire through:
A fire alarm
Smoke
Flames
The smell of burning materials
An announcement from school personnel
Instructions from emergency responders
If a fire alarm sounds, learners should treat the alarm seriously and follow the
established emergency procedure.
Do not assume that an alarm is false simply because you cannot see smoke or
flames.
H. Fire Evacuation Procedure
Step 1: Stay Calm
Do not panic.
Panic can cause people to make unsafe decisions, block exits, or separate from
their group.
Step 2: Follow Instructions
Listen to the teacher, emergency personnel, or designated evacuation leader.
Step 3: Leave Through the Designated Exit
Use the assigned evacuation route.
Do not return to the classroom to retrieve:
Phones
Bags
Books
Toys
Other personal belongings
Personal belongings can be replaced. Your safety is the priority.
Step 4: Move Quickly but Safely
Walk quickly without pushing or running dangerously.
Stay with your group whenever possible.
Step 5: Proceed to the Assembly Area
Go directly to the designated assembly area.
Do not stop in hallways, doorways, stairways, or other locations where you may
block people who are evacuating.
