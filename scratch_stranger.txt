Module 9: Stranger Danger
1. INTRODUCTION
Brief Introduction to the Topic
Stranger danger is a safety topic that teaches children and young learners how to recognize
potentially unsafe situations and respond appropriately when they are approached by
unfamiliar people. However, an important safety principle is that not every stranger is
dangerous, and not every unsafe person looks like a stranger. Children should therefore
learn more than simply “never talk to strangers.” They should learn how to recognize unsafe
behaviors, unsafe requests, uncomfortable situations, and attempts to separate them from
trusted adults.
A person may try to gain a child's trust by being friendly, offering gifts, asking for help,
promising something exciting, or claiming that a parent sent them. The safest response is not
to determine whether the person is “good” or “bad,” but to recognize when a situation feels
unsafe and move toward safety and seek help from a trusted adult.
Why the Topic Is Important
Children may encounter unfamiliar people in many places, including:
o School and school activities
o Parks and playgrounds
o Shopping centers
o Public transportation
o Community areas
o Online games and social media
o Neighborhoods
o Large public events
Teaching children personal-safety skills can help them:
• Recognize potentially unsafe situations.
• Set appropriate boundaries.
• Avoid being isolated from trusted adults.
• Respond to inappropriate requests.
• Seek help quickly.
• Know what information should remain private.
• Respond safely to online and ofMline approaches.
The goal is not to make children afraid of other people. Instead, the goal is to give them
practical skills for making safer decisions.
Age-Appropriate Explanation for Learners
A simple way to explain the lesson is:
“You do not have to talk to everyone who talks to you. If someone makes you uncomfortable,
asks you to keep a secret, asks you to go somewhere without your trusted adult, or tries to
separate you from people who keep you safe, you can say NO, MOVE AWAY, and TELL A
TRUSTED ADULT.”
Children should understand that:
They are allowed to say “No.”
They can move away from someone who makes them uncomfortable.
They do not have to accept gifts or favors from unfamiliar people.
They should not go somewhere with someone without permission from their trusted adult.
They should tell a trusted adult when something feels wrong.
They will not be in trouble for asking for help.
2. Learning Objectives
At the end of the module, learners should be able to:
Objective 1
Identify potentially unsafe situations involving unfamiliar people or inappropriate
requests.
Objective 2
Demonstrate appropriate personal-safety boundaries, including saying “No,” moving away,
and refusing to go somewhere without permission from a trusted adult.
Objective 3
Identify trusted adults and safe places where they can seek assistance when they feel
unsafe, lost, threatened, or uncomfortable.
Objective 4
Apply appropriate safety responses to stranger-related, unsafe-person, and online-contact
scenarios.
3. Interactive Lesson
A. Understanding "Stranger Danger"
The traditional phrase “stranger danger” can be useful for introducing personal safety, but
children need a more complete understanding.
A stranger is simply someone the child does not know.
Being unfamiliar does not automatically mean a person is dangerous.
For example:
A police officer may be unfamiliar but can help during an emergency.
A teacher from another class may be unfamiliar but may be a safe adult within the school.
A doctor may be unfamiliar but may be helping a child receive medical care.
A person in a store may be unfamiliar but may simply be another customer.
Therefore, the lesson should focus on unsafe situations and behaviors, rather than teaching
children that every unfamiliar person is dangerous.
Important Rule
Do not judge safety only by what a person looks like. Pay attention to what the person is
asking you to do.
B. Safe People, Unfamiliar People, and Trusted Adults
Who Is a Trusted Adult?
A trusted adult is someone who:
Cares about the child's safety.
Can help the child when there is a problem.
Respects the child's boundaries.
Can be contacted when the child needs help.
Is known and approved by the child's parent, guardian, or responsible caregiver.
Examples may include:
Parent or guardian
Grandparent
Teacher
School counselor
School administrator
Police officer or other emergency responder when appropriate
Relative approved by the family
Another responsible adult designated by the child's caregiver
Important Concept
A child should know more than one trusted adult.
If one trusted adult is unavailable, the child should know who else they can approach.
C. Recognizing Unsafe Situations
Children should learn to recognize behaviors that may signal danger.
Warning Sign 1: Someone Asks You to Go Somewhere
For example:
“Come with me. I know your parents.”
The child should not go with the person without confirmation and permission from a
trusted adult.
Warning Sign 2: Someone Offers an Unexpected Gift
For example:
“I have candy in my car. Come with me and I'll give you some.”
The child should not go with the person.
A safe response is: “No, thank you.”
Then move toward a trusted adult or safe location.
Warning Sign 3: Someone Asks You to Keep an Unsafe Secret
For example:
“Don't tell your parents that I talked to you.”
Children should understand the difference between:
A Surprise
A temporary event intended to create happiness, such as a birthday surprise.
An Unsafe Secret
A secret someone tells the child to keep from trusted adults because the information
involves inappropriate behavior, threats, gifts, contact, or requests.
A child should be encouraged to tell a trusted adult about an unsafe secret.
Safety Rule:
No adult should make a child feel that they must keep an unsafe secret from the people
responsible for their safety.
D. The "No–Go–Tell" Safety Strategy
A simple strategy for young learners is:
1. NO
Say:
“No!”
or
“I don't want to.”
Children are allowed to say no when someone makes them uncomfortable or asks them to
do something unsafe.
2. GO
Move away.
Go toward:
Parents or guardians
Teachers
School staff
Security personnel
A busy public area
A designated safe place
Children should avoid going to isolated locations with unfamiliar people.
3. TELL
Tell a trusted adult what happened.
Children should continue telling trusted adults until someone listens and helps.
Important Message
If the first adult does not listen, tell another trusted adult. Keep telling until you get help.
E. Personal Boundaries
Personal boundaries are limits that help protect a person's body, privacy, belongings, and
personal information.
Children should understand that:
Their body belongs to them.
They can say no to unwanted contact.
They should respect other people's boundaries too.
They can move away from uncomfortable situations.
They should tell a trusted adult when someone crosses a boundary.
Example
If an unfamiliar person tries to hug a child and the child does not want the hug, the child
can say:
“No, thank you. I don't want a hug.”
The child can then move toward a trusted adult.
F. The "Check First" Rule
Children should learn:
“Check first before going anywhere, accepting something, or following someone.”
For example, if someone says:
“Your mother asked me to pick you up.”
The child should not simply believe the statement.
The child should verify it with their parent, guardian, teacher, or another trusted adult
according to the family's safety plan.
Remember:
A person saying they know your family does not automatically mean they have permission
to take you somewhere.
G. Safe Places
A child who feels unsafe should know where to go.
Examples of safe places may include:
School office
Teacher's classroom
Security or information desk
Police station
Fire station
Trusted relative's home
Designated community safety area
Busy public area with responsible adults
